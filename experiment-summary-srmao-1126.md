# CIFAR-100 unlearning audits against exact accounting: experiment summary (2026/11/26)

## Objective

Quantify how much the tested unlearning audits miss in a CIFAR-100 ridge-regression setting where exact request-specific distinguishability is analytically available, and identify a practical auditor to transfer into the teammates’ shared experimental setting. The paper’s progression is: diagnose audit shortfalls with an exact reference; identify a stronger practical baseline; then compare the teammates’ calibration-efficient and canary-based methods against that baseline in their setting.

The completed experiments support the diagnostic and baseline-selection parts. They do not yet establish that the transferred baseline works well in the teammates’ setting or that the proposed methods improve upon it.

## Main finding

Even the oracle likelihood-ratio score produces a substantially loose lower bound under direct finite-sample inference. At the main CIFAR-100 operating point, \(K=2,000\), its median audited/exact epsilon ratio is **0.467 with direct inference** and **0.901 with Gaussian-based inference** at 10,000 evaluation releases per world. The best median among the tested practical attacks is the forget-set true-class-output score, with ratios **0.161** and **0.378**, respectively. This identifies a candidate baseline to transfer, while also showing that “best among those tested” does not mean close to optimal. At the recovered-utility point, \(K=20,000\), squared loss has the highest median among the tested practical attacks. The ranking therefore depends on the operating point.

## Mechanism and exact reference

We train a ridge-regression head on the full training dataset using \(T\) deterministic full-batch gradient steps from zero. Its objective sums the squared prediction errors and adds ridge regularization. For a fixed deletion request, unlearning starts from this trained head and takes \(K\) noisy full-batch steps on the retained data, adding scaled independent Gaussian noise at every step. Only the final head is released.

The reference first retrains from zero for \(T\) deterministic steps on the retained data, then takes the same \(K\) noisy retain-data steps. It is not a noiseless retrained point estimate. Dataset, request, initialization, feature map, and shared hyperparameters are fixed in this comparison.

We call these two procedures the **unlearning world** and the **retraining-reference world**. One release is a final head generated by either procedure with fresh noise. Audits try to distinguish which world produced a release.

For each fixed request, the two release distributions are Gaussian with the same covariance. This known structure lets the accountant compute their exact epsilon at the chosen delta by numerically evaluating analytic expressions. It is the smallest epsilon that bounds distinguishability in both directions for this pair. It provides an exact request-specific reference, rather than a sampled estimate or a uniform certificate over all deletion requests.

## What the audits measure

Audits use fresh sampled releases from the two worlds, with separate randomness for fitting an attack, calibrating its decision threshold, and evaluating its errors. The accountant and the privileged diagnostic attacks use analytic distributional information. Threshold selection never uses the evaluation labels to optimize the reported bound.

Two inference procedures are reported:

| Procedure | Information used | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Direct Clopper–Pearson (CP) bound | Empirical test errors and binomial confidence bounds | Lower bound on epsilon without a Gaussian-output assumption |
| Gaussian-based bound | Test errors, confidence bounds, and the established equal-covariance Gaussian output laws | Lower bound on epsilon using the known structure of the two release distributions |

Gaussian-based inference is justified by the mechanism’s output law, not by fitting a Gaussian to an arbitrary attack score. Its interpretation must be re-established before transferring it to another mechanism.

The main metric is the **recovery ratio**: audited epsilon divided by exact epsilon for the same deletion request and delta. A ratio of 0.467 means the audit recovers 46.7% of exact epsilon; it does not refer to classification accuracy.

All audit-recovery table entries below are medians of these ratios across the specified requests, not ratios of median epsilon values. All reported exact epsilon values are positive. Each lower bound has nominal 95% confidence individually. Confidence is not simultaneous across the tables, and rows that share sampled models or nested sample budgets are dependent.

## The oracle is optimal, but its audit is still empirical

The oracle score uses the exact mean shift and covariance and achieves the best possible population trade-off between true positives and false positives. Its audited parameter is nevertheless computed from finite calibration and evaluation samples. Confidence intervals, rare errors, and threshold selection leave a gap to exact accounting. The accountant instead evaluates the optimal likelihood-ratio tests analytically to obtain exact epsilon.

Gaussian-based inference uses the known output structure to infer distinguishability from thresholds where errors occur more frequently, which helps it approach accounting more closely. Practical attacks can incur additional losses from score choice, score fitting, and restricted access. These comparisons diagnose limitations; they do not prove a universal impossibility result or an exact additive decomposition of the observed errors.

## CIFAR-100 setup

The experiment fits a \(512\times100\) ridge head to normalized features from a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18, using 50,000 training images and one-hot labels. The backbone is not fine-tuned. Both operating points use \(\lambda=0.1\), \(T=20,000\), \(\eta\approx3.35747034\times10^{-5}\), and \(\delta=2\times10^{-5}\).

| Operating point | Noisy unlearning steps \(K\) | Noise scale \(\sigma_u\) | Role |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Original | 2,000 | 3.1038583 | Main benchmark for the current paper storyline |
| Recovered utility | 20,000 | 0.2508395 | Completed robustness comparison |

Exact accounting covers 1,209 requests across deletion sizes 100, 200, and 500 at the original operating point. Auditing covers 11 selected size-500 requests: median and hardest requests within four untargeted families, plus three gradient-based targeted requests. The same 11 request indices are used at the recovered-utility point. Selection roles refer to the original operating point; they need not remain median or hardest at the later one.

For each request, the CIFAR audits use \(N=1,000\) and \(10,000\) releases per world in each of the fitting, calibration, and evaluation splits. Attacks share releases, and the smaller budget uses prefixes of the larger banks. Each operating point has 110 request/attack/budget rows.

## CIFAR-100 audit results

The following table uses \(N=10,000\). Oracle likelihood ratio and known mean difference use analytic distributional information. Learned mean difference uses separate fitting samples and does not estimate a full covariance matrix. The output-score attack averages the raw ridge output assigned to each forget example’s true class; it is not the joint sign-vector auditor’s logit-confidence score.

| Attack | \(K=2,000\): direct | \(K=2,000\): Gaussian-based | \(K=20,000\): direct | \(K=20,000\): Gaussian-based |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Oracle likelihood ratio | 0.467 | 0.901 | 0.450 | 0.913 |
| Known mean difference | 0.457 | 0.905 | 0.450 | 0.887 |
| Learned mean difference | 0.077 | 0.185 | 0.094 | 0.236 |
| Forget-set true-class output | **0.161** | **0.378** | 0.166 | 0.303 |
| Forget-set squared loss | 0.053 | 0.139 | **0.206** | **0.388** |

Bold entries identify the highest median among the three tested practical attacks at each operating point and estimator. They do not establish statistically significant dominance or a winner on every request.

**Baseline to transfer:** evaluate the true-class-output score in the teammates’ shared setting and retain squared loss as a comparator, since loss leads at the recovered-utility point. These experiments support a baseline candidate, but do not establish one consistently strongest practical attack or guarantee that its ranking will transfer.

At the original point, the oracle direct/exact ratio ranges from 0.432 to 0.505 across requests, whereas its Gaussian-based ratio ranges from 0.867 to 0.982. The practical output score reaches only 0.104–0.235 with direct inference. At \(N=1,000\), learned mean difference and squared loss both have median zero lower bounds under both estimators. Zero means inconclusive auditing, not zero distinguishability.

## Model accuracy and comparison limits

The original deterministic head has 62.82% test accuracy. The original noisy-release utility evaluation gives 9.53% mean accuracy with a standard deviation of 0.54 percentage points over 20 noise draws. This low utility must accompany the \(K=2,000\) findings.

A validation-based sweep selected \(K=20,000\) as the first tested point within five percentage points of the noiseless retain-reference accuracy. The completed audit at this point measures utility using 64 independent unlearned models per request. Mean test accuracies range from 60.02% to 60.70%, with median 60.59%. The oracle and practical audit gaps persist, so the phenomenon is observed at useful overall accuracy as well as at the original low-utility point.

Both \(K\) and the noise scale change. The comparison does not isolate the effect of iteration count, and exact epsilon is recomputed separately for every request and operating point. The noise calibration targets typical random-request difficulty, not equal leakage for all requests.

## Supporting CIFAR-100 experiments

Controlled CIFAR-100 experiments examine concentration, gradient cancellation, curvature, fractional deletion, matched requests, spectral reversals, and search budgets. They show substantial request heterogeneity and limitations of first-order approximations, but they are not needed to establish the central auditing result.

Keep these findings in supporting material or later research. The four-page main text should prioritize the exact reference, audit comparisons, baseline choice, and integration with the teammates’ results. New distribution-discrepancy bounds, explanations of deletion difficulty, and new worst-case certificates are outside the current empirical contribution.

## Validation and limits

Independent simulator diagnostics were checked against the analytic Gaussian laws. Both CIFAR audits validated all 11 selected requests; deterministic GD and its closed form agreed to approximately \(1.2\times10^{-11}\). No lower bound exceeded its exact target in either set of 110 CIFAR audit rows, for either estimator. These are observed consistency checks, not independent coverage trials or proof of correctness.

The conclusions concern fixed requests, the specified final release, and fixed shared hyperparameters. They do not establish protection for prior model releases, adaptive deletion sequences, or the full data-dependent hyperparameter-selection pipeline. The selected CIFAR requests are not an exhaustive worst-case search. Results do not establish that every practical auditor must remain loose or that the candidate baseline will transfer successfully.
